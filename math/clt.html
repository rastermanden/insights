What you're seeing

THE CLAIMPick any population with mean μ and finite SD σ. Draw n values, average them, and call that one sample mean. Repeat many times. The histogram of those sample means tends toward a normal distribution centred at μ with SD σ/√n — even when the population itself is skewed, bimodal, or discrete.

TRY n = 1With sample size 1, the "average" is just one observation, so the second chart has the same shape as the population. No magic yet.

TRY n = 5Already the exponential's long right tail starts pulling in. The bimodal's two peaks merge into one bump. The dice histogram becomes triangular.

TRY n = 30+The histogram closely tracks the dashed normal curve in every case. Thirty is the rule-of-thumb threshold textbooks use for "the CLT has kicked in."

SHRINKAGEThe spread of the sample means is σ/√n. To halve the spread, you need four times the sample size. That's why precision is expensive — and why the bottom histogram becomes a tighter spike as you slide n upward.

WHY IT MATTERSConfidence intervals, p-values, A/B tests, polling margins of error, and the "± 1.96 standard errors" recipe all rest on this theorem. The Bayes, Risk/NNT, and P-Value demos in this set quietly assume a normal sampling distribution — this is where it comes from.
