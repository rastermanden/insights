How to read it

P-VALUEThe probability of seeing a difference this large (or larger) if the treatment really had no effect. A small p-value (typically < 0.05) means the observed result would be unlikely under "no effect" — so we reject that explanation. It is not the probability that the treatment works, nor the probability the result is a fluke.

95% CIThe range of effect sizes the data are compatible with. If we repeated the study many times, about 95% of such intervals would contain the true effect. A narrow CI means the estimate is precise; a wide CI means we don't know much yet.

CROSSING 0When a CI for a difference includes 0 (or a CI for a ratio like risk ratio / hazard ratio includes 1), the data don't rule out "no effect." In abstracts you'll see this paired with p > 0.05 and phrases like "no significant difference."

WATCH OUTStatistical significance is not clinical importance. Try the "Tiny effect, huge sample" scenario: a trivial 0.5-unit difference becomes "highly significant" with enough subjects. Always look at the size of the effect and the width of the CI — not just the p-value.

EXAMPLEAn abstract reading "the drug reduced systolic BP by 5.2 mmHg (95% CI 1.3 to 9.1, p = 0.013)" tells you: the best estimate is 5.2 mmHg, the true effect is plausibly anywhere from 1.3 to 9.1, and a result this far from 0 would be uncommon (~1.3% chance) under the null.
