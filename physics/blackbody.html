What is a blackbody?

An idealised object that absorbs all incident radiation and re-emits a thermal spectrum that depends only on its temperature — not on its material, chemistry, or surface texture. Stars, glowing embers, tungsten filaments, and even human skin are well-approximated as blackbodies (scaled by an emissivity \( \varepsilon \le 1 \)).

Formula

Planck's law gives the spectral radiance \( B_\lambda \) of a blackbody at temperature \( T \) per unit wavelength:

\[ B_\lambda(T) = \frac{2 h c^2}{\lambda^5} \cdot \frac{1}{e^{hc/(\lambda k_B T)} - 1} \]

Two consequences fall out by integrating or differentiating:

  • Wien's displacement law: \( \lambda_\text{max} = b / T \), with \( b \approx 2.898\times10^{-3}\,\text{m·K} \). Hotter objects peak at shorter wavelengths.
  • Stefan-Boltzmann law: \( P = \sigma T^4 \). Total radiated power per unit area scales as \( T^4 \) — double the temperature, get sixteen times the power.

Why it matters

  • It launched quantum mechanics. Classical physics predicted infinite radiation at short wavelengths — the "ultraviolet catastrophe". In 1900 Planck resolved it by postulating that energy is exchanged in discrete quanta \( E = h\nu \). Every later quantum theory traces back to this curve.
  • It tells us the temperature of stars. We cannot put a thermometer on the Sun, but fitting its spectrum (or just its peak wavelength) to Planck's law gives \( T \approx 5778 \) K. The same method dates cool red dwarfs (~3000 K) and hot blue giants (~30,000 K). Stellar classification (O B A F G K M) is essentially a temperature ranking.
  • It is the fingerprint of the Big Bang. The most perfect blackbody ever measured is the cosmic microwave background at \( T = 2.725 \) K — a faint relic glow filling all of space. Its near-perfect Planck shape, mapped by the COBE, WMAP and Planck satellites, is the strongest evidence we have for a hot, dense early universe.
  • It sets Earth's climate. Our planet's equilibrium temperature comes from balancing absorbed sunlight against thermal re-emission \( \propto T^4 \). The greenhouse effect is just selective absorption of that outgoing infrared blackbody radiation; every climate model is built on this energy budget.
  • It explains why incandescent bulbs are inefficient. A 2800 K filament peaks deep in the infrared, so roughly 95% of its radiated power leaves as heat instead of visible light. LEDs win by sidestepping thermal emission entirely. The same physics imposes the Shockley-Queisser limit on solar-cell efficiency, tied to the Sun's 5778 K spectrum.
  • It enables non-contact thermometry. Infrared ear thermometers, thermal cameras, and industrial pyrometers all invert Planck's law — measure the spectrum, recover the temperature — to read fevers, find heat leaks in buildings, or monitor furnaces from a safe distance.

Try the presets above: a candle glows red because its spectrum peaks well past 700 nm; the Sun looks white because its peak sits in the middle of the visible band; Sirius appears blue-white because its peak is pushed into the near-UV.
